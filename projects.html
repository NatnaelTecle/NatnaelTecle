AI-Powered Revenue Forecasting App for an Audit Committee

ITEC 3155 / ACTG 4155 · Spring 2025 · Python, Streamlit, ARIMA/ARIMAX · Partner project, presented in a group of four

Business problem
Auditors need early warning when a company's reported revenue looks too good. This project asked: how can an audit committee evaluate the risk that Starbucks has overstated revenue, using a forecast as the benchmark?
My role
I built the forecasting engine and the data pipeline. That included fitting the ARIMAX model from my earlier project, pulling live CPI data from FRED, and connecting both to a user input for expected revenue growth. My partner focused on the page layout and charts, and we split the AI summary and the written documentation.
New insight
I added store count as a new variable. It let us ask whether revenue growth came from opening more stores or from each store earning more.
Challenges
The live CPI data arrived in a different frequency than quarterly revenue, so I had to align the two series before the model would run. Early AI summaries were too generic for an audit committee, so I rewrote the prompts to require specific numbers and a clear risk statement, then edited the output by hand. I also had to explain ARIMA without jargon.
Enhancement
Risk-flagging logic that marks a quarter when actual revenue sits well above the forecast range, so the committee sees where to look first.
Artifacts
Add screenshots of the app, the forecast chart, a link to the .py file, and the presentation slides.
Business value and outcome
Actual revenue tracked the forecast closely for most quarters, but the gap widened in recent periods when store growth outpaced revenue per store. That supported a moderate-risk conclusion: the growth is explainable, but worth testing for aggressive recognition. The app turns a statistical model into a risk signal a non-technical committee can read in one chart and one message.

IT Audit and Cybersecurity Readiness Assessment: Fast Freddy's Furniture Depot

ACTG 4575 · Team simulation of a client engagement · Audit program, workpapers, findings, and executive presentation

Business problem
Fast Freddy's, a Denver furniture manufacturer with a growing web business, runs everything on one SAP ERP system hosted in an Omaha datacenter. Executives wanted to know how ready their cybersecurity was, and the financial statement auditors needed to know whether they could rely on the company's IT general controls.
My role
I helped draft the audit program before our interview with the CIO and took the lead on the user account management and password settings sections, from test steps through workpapers. I also helped write up findings and presented the access control results to the client's executives.
Challenges
Interview time and follow-up requests were limited, so we prepared a draft audit program and a question list in advance and used only our allowed follow-ups. We also had to fold in the datacenter and backup results from another audit team so the program and findings read as one engagement.
Artifacts
Link a sanitized audit program, a sample workpaper, and the presentation.
Business value and outcome
Our findings included user accounts that stayed active after employees left, password settings weaker than company policy, and program changes without documented approval. We rated cybersecurity readiness as developing, with clear gaps in monitoring and testing. We concluded that the audit team could not rely on the IT general controls this period without expanded substantive testing, and we gave management a prioritized list of fixes, starting with access removal.

Coastline Landscaping Database Design

INFO 3140 · SQL, entity relationship modeling · Individual project

Business problem
Coastline, a growing West Coast landscaping company, spread its resources too thin across several states. Management wanted to find its most and least popular services, identify the regions with the highest demand, and focus on smaller markets before restructuring the business.
My role
I did all of it: wrote the business rules, designed the five-entity model (Client, Invoice, Property, Service, and Property Service), drew the ER diagram in Lucidchart, built and loaded the database in SQL, and wrote the queries. Because properties and services are many-to-many, I resolved that relationship with the Property Service table.
Challenges
Keeping the ER diagram and the actual tables consistent, getting cardinality right, and making sure every foreign key pointed to a real primary key. The many-to-many relationship was the part I had to rework before the design was correct.
Queries
Four queries, each with a business purpose: services ranked by the number of properties served; clients whose total billing exceeded a threshold (GROUP BY with HAVING); properties with no services recorded, using an outer join; and revenue by region.
Artifacts
Add the ER diagram image, a link to the .sql file, and a screenshot of a query result.
Business value and outcome
The queries showed that a few core services, such as irrigation maintenance, drove most of the activity, while specialty services like pest control were requested far less often. That is the evidence management needs to decide which offerings to strengthen and which to phase out.
